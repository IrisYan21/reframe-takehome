# Pass 3 — Community threads: the "why" behind the ratings

**Author:** Iris Yan · **Date:** 2026-09-11 · **Source:** 73 Reddit threads, 836 comments, ~157,000 words, collected manually from r/stopdrinking, r/dryalcoholics, r/SoberCurious and adjacent subs. Quote bank: `data/pass3_quote_bank.csv` (52 coded quotes).

**Why this pass exists.** Store reviews say what broke. They are written after the fact, by people who stayed long enough to have an opinion. Reddit is where people talk *before* they download, while they are shopping, and after they quietly stopped opening the app. That last group never leaves a review, and it is the group onboarding actually loses.

**Corpus composition.**

## Finding 1 — The trigger is an accumulation, not an incident

The thing people describe on the day they act is almost never a catastrophe. It is a slow erosion that crossed a line, and the language is consistently flat and tired rather than dramatic.

> "I didn't really have a rock bottom moment, I just got really tired of the endless loop of drinking and hangovers." — T35
> "There was no rock bottom, just a lot of really low days." — T55
> "I quit well before 'Rock Bottom.' I hated how I felt in the morning, but more importantly I hated how often I craved alcohol." — T55

The rock-bottom narrative is not just absent, it is actively resented. It is the single most contested idea in the corpus, because people read it as an entry requirement they do not meet yet.

> "You don't need to wait until your life has an obvious rock bottom moment." — T53
> "I never hit a 'rock bottom' and no one would have ever said I had a problem." — T53

When a concrete trigger does appear, it is a body signal, not a life event. Sleep and anxiety dominate: 282 sentences mention sleep, 106 mention personal anxiety, often as the thing that finally made the cost legible.

> "no matter how much or how little I drink, my sleep is always garbage if I drink alcohol that day." — T63
> "The hangxiety was too much in the end and I was sick of feeling so embarrassed of myself all the time." — T29
> "my anxiety and BP dropped significantly after just two weeks." — T67

**Onboarding implication.** The opening question should ask what they have noticed, not how bad it has gotten. Severity framing at the top of the funnel disqualifies the exact person Reframe sells to.

---

## Finding 2 — The first real task is not goal-setting, it is label negotiation

Before anyone picks a goal, they are busy deciding whether they are allowed to be here. Identity language appears in 94 sentences, and it runs in both directions: people arguing they are not that bad, and people being told by others that they are not that bad.

> "I hate the term alcoholic - I think it prevents people from getting help sooner than they should." — T52
> "I used to obsess over whether or not I 'count' as an 'alcoholic', now I don't even sweat that and just accept that it's a net negative for me and that's all that matters!" — T54
> "one of the harder parts of quitting, for me, was what people said: 'your not an alcoholic' 'you're being dramatic'." — T55

The self-portrait that recurs is high-functioning and outwardly fine, which matches the profile we set for the 35 to 55 buyer.

> "My drinking wasn't anything spectacular - 3-5 glasses of wine nightly. Everything OK on the outside (job, husband & kids, family & friends, fitness) But...I wasn't ok on the inside." — T57

**Onboarding implication.** An intake that reads as a diagnostic quiz forces the label decision at the worst possible moment. The winning move is to make the label unnecessary: qualify people on what they want to change, never on how bad they are.

## Finding 3 — The goal vocabulary is unstable, and the copy has to survive that

Raw frequency across the corpus:

| Term | Mentions |
| --- | --- |
| sober / sobriety | 714 |
| quit | 364 |
| stop drinking | 144 |
| moderate / moderation | 124 |
| day 1 | 65 |
| dry january | 50 |
| alcohol-free / AF | 28 |
| cut back / cut down | 18 |
| take a break | 3 |

Two cautions before using this table. These subs self-select for quitting, so the abstinence words are inflated relative to Reframe's actual buyer, 70% of whom arrive wanting to cut back. And "moderation" is rarely used as a goal here. It is used as a failed project and an ongoing mental tax.

> "I'm sick of the constant mental conflict of trying to stop or moderate." — T56
> "when your thoughts aren't cosumbed by moderation (Schrodingers alcoholic cat), it will be a huge mental relief." — T53

What the corpus shows clearly is movement. People change goal mid-journey, in both directions, and the app has to hold that without making them feel they failed.

> "When I was in moderation mode, I found it incredibly helpful... I actually was able to reduce from 30 drinks to 12 a week using this app. I stopped drinking fully back in February though." — T4
> "I did reframe for dry January. It was truly great for me." — T4

**Onboarding implication.** Set the goal as a provisional, editable setting rather than a declared identity, and mirror the user's own word back. Time-boxed framings like Dry January and 30 days are the lowest-commitment entry point and they convert into longer runs.

## Finding 4 — The silent churn is modular disengagement, not deletion

Nobody in this corpus says they deleted Reframe in anger. What they describe is dropping features one at a time until the subscription no longer justifies itself. This is the closest proxy we have for the churned user who never writes a review.

> "I like the drink tracker and the daily tasks...I can't stand the meetings so I yeeted those and soon after the community forums. For me it isn't worth the subscription" — T1
> "there's a lot to learn on it but I'm not to into the meetings of community forums either." — T1
> "the first few times going sober, i did really enjoy learning more about the biology behind addiction but have not found myself accessing that area for a while now." — T14

The community and meetings features split the audience hardest. For some they are the whole product. For others they are the reason to leave.

> "The meetings are a lifesaver" — T1
> "I also don't need some app community." — T14

One Reframe-specific mechanic is named as a reason to prefer a competitor: the sober-date reset after a slip.

> "I like how it rolls with me, I don't have to enter a sober date every Day 1, unlike Reframe." — T7

**Onboarding implication.** Onboarding currently sells one bundled product. It should let people opt into the two or three surfaces they actually want and hide the rest, and the counter must survive a slip without erasing the user's history.

## Finding 5 — The category objections are price, privacy and effort

Price is the loudest, and the comparison set is free.

> "it is however prohibitively pricey - i got mine heavily discounted." — T14
> "I know reframe is good but I can't afford to pay for it right now." — T6
> "I'm currently doing the 7-day free trial (a year is $99) so I want to see if it's worth the investment! It seems to have a lot of great tools but I'm not sure where to start besides daily reflections." — T4

"I Am Sober" is named 31 times against Reframe's 23, and more than 20 distinct apps appear, most of them free. The category's default price is zero, so the paywall is not competing against nothing.

Privacy and account friction come next, and they are specific and unprompted. 34 sentences touch account, data or privacy.

> "I really value not needing an account. All my data should be local." — T14
> "I didn't want to do data sharing on a sobriety focused app, TBH." — T7
> "I also agree with other posters about forgoing some involved account registration process." — T14

Then effort, which is the direct echo of Pass 1's survey-length problem.

> "Many are confusing if you mess up and need a reset. I already feel bad I dont need to spend 5 to 10 minutes to log it." — T14
> "I have tried with Excel and notes but I am too lazy to work on those after a week. A simple app which you put a number and registers in a calendar would be awesome." — T5

And the hardest objection of all, the one that questions whether the category should exist: some people read the act of tracking as an admission.

> "once your registering drinks in app, it's clear you a larger problem." — T5

## What this means for the onboarding redesign

**Mindset at first download.** Ambivalent and tired, not committed. They have decided to look, not to change. They are simultaneously asking "is this for someone like me" and "am I overreacting." They expect to be judged and are scanning for the first sign of it.

**What they expect.** To be met at the level of a symptom, not a diagnosis. To find out fast whether this is worth money when free alternatives exist. To not be asked to declare an identity in the first minute.

**Interactions they are happy to use.** Short, daily, low-stakes and repeatable. The mechanics people volunteer as habit-forming are the morning check-in, the pledge, the drink log that takes seconds, and a one-tap tool for the moment a craving hits. What they refuse is a long intake, mandatory community, and anything that takes five to ten minutes to correct after a mistake.

**Information and services they are seeking.** In priority order from the corpus: a number that goes up (days, money, calories, drinks avoided), an explanation of what alcohol is doing to their brain and sleep, a way through a specific craving right now, and optional human contact. The science content is a genuine differentiator and is cited as a motivator on day 3.

**What keeps them in.** Visible accumulation, early physical wins around sleep and anxiety, and forgiveness after a slip. A counter that resets to zero on day one of a relapse is the mechanic most likely to end the relationship, because it deletes the evidence of progress at the moment they most need it.

**What raises satisfaction.** Letting them keep the parts they like and switch off the rest. Custom milestones that mean something personal. Reflecting their own words and numbers back to them. Proving value before asking for the card.

**Six changes this supports**

1. Open on what they noticed, not how much they drink. Symptom first, quantity later.
2. Replace the identity question with a goal question, phrased in their words: cut back, take a break, do a dry month, quit.
3. Make the goal editable from day one and say so in the interface.
4. Cut the intake to what personalization genuinely consumes, and show a personalized artifact before the paywall, not 22 seconds before it.
5. Let people choose their surfaces during onboarding. Tracker only is a legitimate configuration.
6. Redesign the slip. Keep total sober days alongside current streak so a relapse edits the record instead of erasing it.

## Limitations

Self-selected communities, so the sample skews toward people who quit rather than moderate, and the vocabulary table over-represents abstinence language. No verified ages, so this corpus cannot confirm the 35 to 55 band. Reframe is named in only 23 places, so the Reframe-specific claims in Finding 4 rest on a small base and are labeled as such. All quotes are public posts, reproduced with usernames as posted and thread numbers traceable in the quote bank.

**What I would test with real users.** A five-minute moderated session on two intake openings, symptom-first versus quantity-first, measuring drop-off and self-reported judgment. And a slip simulation to see what a reset counter does to intent to continue.
